What we take responsibility for

Our work covers technical SEO audits, keyword-to-page decisions, content briefs, on-page fixes, local search support and reporting. We start with the pages that matter, the work the client can approve, and the evidence a reviewer needs to sign off.

Our focus is clear scope and reviewable output. Before work begins, a useful proposal should identify the site or client accounts in scope, the work products to be delivered, the person who approves changes, and the evidence required to accept each task.

Who owns the relationship and the website?

For agencies, you keep the client relationship, strategy commitments, and the final sign-off. For businesses, your team keeps decisions about services, claims, legal requirements, and publishing. The outsourced delivery team works on agreed tasks and provides documentation for your reviewer.

We recommend named, least-privilege logins rather than shared passwords. Publishing permissions, subcontracting, confidentiality, and access removal should be agreed in writing for each engagement.

How to evaluate us before expanding a contract

  1. Inspect the evidence: review dated ranking observations at their source and understand what the metrics do and do not show.
  2. Examine a sample: look for issue reproduction steps, source facts, owners, and acceptance checks rather than a generic checklist.
  3. Agree a pilot: define a bounded first work cycle with reviewers, dependencies, and revision rules.
  4. Decide based on fit: use the pilot's completeness, communication, and rework needs to determine whether an ongoing workstream makes sense.

These steps are practical procurement guidance. Ranking snapshots alone do not establish which SEO activities produced a result, and sample templates are labeled as examples rather than confidential client files.

What to expect in the proposal

Ask for the contracting entity and delivery contact, confirmed scope, pricing exclusions, access requirements, acceptance criteria, confidentiality terms if needed, reporting sources, and an escalation path. The final offer should answer those questions before you grant access to client assets.

Five checks before you hand over a website

Ask these during an introductory conversation. The answers should be recorded in the agreement, not left as assumptions after access is granted.

  1. Who can approve changes? Name one person on each side. Editors can prepare work without receiving unrestricted publication rights.
  2. Which environment is safe to touch? Separate read-only analytics, staging access and production permissions.
  3. What does “finished” mean? Require a live URL, before/after evidence where possible, a reviewer and outstanding exceptions.
  4. Who speaks to your client? For white-label work, state whether direct contact is prohibited or separately authorized.
  5. How can you leave? Confirm asset ownership, document export, access revocation and delivery of outstanding work.

Five things a real outsourcing agreement should make clear.

A polished proposal is not enough. An agency or website owner should be able to identify the accountable people, access boundaries and the evidence used to accept work. These are the questions to settle before sharing a login.

DecisionWhat to get in writing
Contracting partyLegal business entity, billing contact and the person responsible for delivery. Confirm those details independently.
AccessNamed, least-privilege accounts, who can publish changes and how access will be revoked when the engagement ends.
Work acceptanceTarget URLs or assets, required output, reviewer, evidence and the exact condition that makes a task complete.
ConfidentialitySubcontractor permissions, permitted tools and how client or customer data must be handled.
Handoff or exitOwnership of briefs, tickets, reports and files, plus a way to transfer unfinished work without losing the audit trail.

Before agreeing to a larger retainer, test one representative deliverable against this checklist. The proposal identifies the contracting party, delivery owner and escalation contact before any client access is provided.
